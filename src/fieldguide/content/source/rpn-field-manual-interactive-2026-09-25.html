01
The bigger picture

Our Ecosystem

Before anything about the app itself, it helps to know why seiGEN Commerce exists at all, and where you fit in it. This is the picture worth carrying with you into every vendor's shop.

Zimbabwe's hustle economy

Most businesses in Zimbabwe are informal — the tuck shops, the market stalls, the one-person operations. That's not a small or marginal slice of the economy: informal trade is a major contributor to Zimbabwe's GDP and one of the country's biggest sources of employment. This isn't unique to Zimbabwe either — it's the pattern across much of Africa.

The question that matters most for these businesses isn't whether they can survive — they already do, every day. It's whether they can grow: keep proper records, make better decisions, and turn a hustle into a lasting business.

Why seiGEN Commerce Lite exists

seiGEN Commerce Lite was built to grow alongside African businesses — Zimbabwe's informal traders included — rather than asking them to become something else first. Simple enough to onboard in one sitting, but built to carry a business as it scales.

How the pieces fit together

NameWhat it is
seiGEN Commerce Infrastructure (SCI)The umbrella technology infrastructure behind the whole product family.
Digital Commerce ZWLicensed by SCI to distribute its products in Zimbabwe — including through the RPN network.
RPN (you)The face of SCI in the field. The street soldiers tasked with onboarding vendors onto the SaaS.
seiGEN Commerce LiteThe core POS product — what you install and support day to day.
iTred VPDThe customer-facing app that grows out of Commerce Lite's core, giving vendors' products visibility to buyers beyond their own shop.

That last connection is worth sitting with: when you help a vendor use the marketing export inside Commerce Lite, you're not just teaching a feature — you're plugging that vendor into iTred VPD, and into a bigger market than the one walking past their door.

FIELD TIP

When a vendor asks “who are you with?”, the honest answer is: you're an RPN partner, representing Digital Commerce ZW, who distribute seiGEN Commerce Infrastructure's products — starting with Commerce Lite.

Why this matters to you

Every vendor you onboard is one more informal business with real records, real visibility, and a real shot at growing. That's the actual job underneath the onboarding checklist.

03
Your role

Who You Are Out There

You're an independent partner — not a Digital Commerce employee. You're paid on commission, and you work one city: the one you were verified for. That independence is real, and so is the responsibility that comes with it. When you're in front of a vendor, you are Digital Commerce — and by extension, seiGEN Commerce Infrastructure — to them.

Your two ongoing jobs

  1. Vendor support — you're the first person a vendor calls when something's confusing or broken.
  2. Coordinating stocktake cycles — keeping vendors on a regular counting rhythm so their stock numbers stay trustworthy.

The line you don't cross

Stocktakes are the one place people most often get the boundary wrong, so be precise about it: the vendor's stock, the vendor's shop, the vendor's count. You coordinate — you don't do the counting for them. Your part is scheduling it, prepping them for it, and helping capture the result into the app once they've counted. If you find yourself physically counting stock item by item, you've stepped into their core work, not supported it.

DON'T

Don't take over a routine stocktake because it's faster if you just do it yourself. It isn't your job, and a vendor who never learns to count their own stock never actually owns the process.

FIELD TIP

For a quick cycle check-in, a good rule of thumb: if you're holding the clipboard and counting, you're doing their job; if you're holding the phone and helping them enter what they counted, you're doing yours. A full, organized stocktake is different — Chapter 11 covers how you run one of those.

04
Your conduct

Presenting Yourself, Professionally

As an RPN, you represent Digital Commerce every time you meet a vendor, visit a business, conduct a stocktake, provide training, or solve a problem. The way you dress, speak, organize yourself and open a conversation all become part of the vendor's experience of the company — before they've even seen the app.

REMEMBER

Before a vendor understands what you're selling, they first see how you present yourself and how you behave.

Dressing matters, even without a budget

Your clothing doesn't need to be expensive. It needs to be clean, neat, appropriate, presentable, and suitable for the work you're doing that day. If you're in a formal meeting, dress for a formal meeting. If you're conducting a physical stocktake, stay neat while allowing yourself to work safely and comfortably.

Wear clean, neat clothing and shoes, keep your hair neat, maintain good hygiene, and avoid anything dirty, damaged, or out of place for business. A clean, simple, well-presented appearance beats expensive clothing worn untidily — every time.

Be organized before you arrive

Professionalism isn't only about appearance. Before you get to a vendor, you should already know who you're meeting, the name of the business, why you're visiting, what's already been agreed, and what you need from them.

SAY THIS

“Prepare yourself → prepare your information → prepare your tools → prepare your questions → then visit the vendor.”

Depending on the task, carry: your phone and a charger or power bank, a notebook or approved digital notes, the relevant Digital Commerce forms, the stocktake spreadsheet where it applies, a pen, and any identification or documents the assignment needs.

DON'T

Don't arrive and start working out what you're supposed to do. An organized person prepares before the meeting, not during it.

How you speak

Your tone should be respectful, calm, clear, friendly, confident and professional — not arrogant, and not afraid to speak. You're representing a business, so speak with confidence while staying respectful.

Don't try to impress a vendor with complicated language. Your job is to make them understand you, not to sound clever. Compare:

Instead of thisSay this
“We shall facilitate the implementation of an integrated inventory management solution subsequent to the stock reconciliation exercise.”“After we complete and verify the stocktake, we will load the stock information into the POS.”

Simple English is professional English when it actually communicates. When you explain something: say what you're going to do, explain why, explain what you need from the vendor, ask if they understand, then give them room to ask questions.

Choose your words carefully

Instead of thisSay this
“Give me your stock list.”“Could you please provide the stock list so we can compare it with what we're collecting?”
“You have to do this.”“This is what we need to complete the process.”
“You are wrong.”“Let's check this information together and see where the difference comes from.”
“I don't know.”“Let me confirm that information and come back to you.”

Starting a conversation

Don't open with problems, prices or demands. Greet the person properly, make eye contact where it's culturally appropriate, smile naturally, and introduce yourself before you get to business.

SAY THIS

“Good morning. My name is [Name]. I am an RPN with Digital Commerce. Thank you for giving me the opportunity to meet with you.”

“I am here to discuss your onboarding and the stocktake process with you.”

“Before we begin, I would like to understand your business and what you would like us to help you with.”

That structure — greet, introduce, thank, explain your purpose, ask questions, listen, explain, confirm understanding, agree the next step — turns a meeting into a conversation instead of an interrogation.

Learn to listen

A good RPN doesn't talk the whole time. When a vendor explains a problem, let them finish. Listen for what they need, what they've already tried, what's missing, and what they expect Digital Commerce to do. If you don't understand something, ask — that's better than pretending to.

SAY THIS

“Could you please explain that part again?”

“When you say the stock isn't matching, what exactly is happening?”

Don't argue, and be careful with promises

If a vendor disagrees with you, stay calm and explain your reasoning respectfully rather than getting defensive. If you're not authorized to commit to something, don't — say you'll confirm it first. That protects you and Digital Commerce.

DON'T

Never make a promise you're not authorized or able to keep.

Body language says as much as your words

Stand or sit properly, pay attention, and keep a calm expression. Avoid constantly checking your phone, folding your arms defensively, showing impatience, or laughing at a vendor's mistake. If you genuinely need your phone for the work, say so — “Let me just check that information on the system” — rather than silently looking at it while they're speaking.

During a stocktake, conduct matters even more

You may be working inside a vendor's business for hours. Dress neatly, arrive on time, respect their property, handle products carefully, keep the working area organized, avoid taking photographs unless it's required and authorized, keep vendor information confidential, and treat their employees respectfully. Remember: you're working inside someone else's business.

How to end a visit

Don't just leave once the work is done. Summarize what happened, ask if anything needs clarifying, and thank them.

SAY THIS

“Today we completed the first batch of the stocktake. The information has been checked and we have agreed on the items that need clarification. We will continue with the next batch on the agreed date.”

“Is there anything you would like me to clarify before I leave?”

“Thank you for your time. We appreciate your cooperation.”

RPN GOLDEN RULE

Look professional. Speak respectfully. Listen carefully. Be prepared. Tell the truth. Do what you say you will do. You're often the human face of Digital Commerce to a vendor — every visit should leave them feeling they're dealing with someone organized, respectful, knowledgeable and serious about business.

05
Relationships

Selling Without Selling

Nobody wants to be sold to. Every vendor you meet has heard a pitch before. What works instead is starting from a problem they already have.

How to open

SAY THIS

“Quick question — at the end of the day, do you actually know what you sold, or are you working it out from memory?”

“How do you currently know when you're running low on something before it's gone?”

Both of those are real problems most small shops live with every day. You're not describing features; you're naming something they already feel.

Reading hesitation

If a vendor slows down or goes quiet, that's not a signal to talk faster. Stop and ask what's actually on their mind — it's often something small: a worry about losing existing habits, a worry about cost, a worry about their staff coping with something new. Answer the real thing, not the thing you assumed.

SAY THIS

“What's the part of this that worries you most?”

Trust is built after the sale, not during it

Anyone can sound convincing for twenty minutes. What actually earns a vendor's trust is showing up when you said you would, and being reachable after they've handed over their business's daily numbers to a new tool. That reputation follows you to the next vendor in the same neighbourhood.

FIELD TIP

Vendors talk to each other. One vendor who feels well supported is worth more to you long-term than one who was just closed quickly.

Core skill

Onboarding A New Vendor

Onboarding is the highest-stakes hour you'll spend with any vendor. Get it right and they're self-sufficient within days. Get it wrong and you'll be fielding basic questions for months.

Before you arrive

Confirm this firstWhy
Their device and browserThe app is designed to run in a standard browser, not inside a chat app's file preview.
Timing of the first internet connectionOnly the very first load needs a connection — after that it works offline. Plan around their signal.
Main vs Remote branchDecide this before you start — it's awkward to change after setup.

During the visit

  1. Walk through setup screen by screen, narrating what you're doing and why — don't do it silently.
  2. Move into a guided first sale. This is the part people get wrong: your hands stay off their phone. You talk, they tap.
  3. Before you leave, have them repeat one full sale and one EOD step completely unassisted, while you watch and say nothing unless they get stuck.
DON'T

If you've done most of the tapping by the time you leave, they haven't actually learned it — and you'll be back sooner than you'd like.

FIELD TIP

Narrate like you're training someone to drive, not like you're demonstrating a gadget: “now you'd tap here because...”, not just “watch this.”

Core skill

Initial Setup, Done Right

Setup mistakes are the ones that cost the most later, because they're awkward to undo once a vendor has started using the app day to day. Get these decisions right at the start.

Main vs Remote branch — decide by role, not by device

This trips people up constantly: the decision is about how the vendor's business is structured, not what device is in front of you. A laptop can be a Remote branch just as easily as a phone can. Ask about the business first, the device second.

If the vendor...Then it's usually...
Runs one shop, one tillMain branch
Has more than one location or till reporting back to a central pointMain at the central point, Remote at each other location

The first connection

The app needs the internet for its very first load only. After that, it runs offline. Plan the timing of that first load around wherever the vendor actually has reliable signal — don't assume their shop does.

FIELD TIP

If you're not sure whether a business should be one branch or several, ask: “if I stood in each location, would each one need its own till record?” That usually settles it.

09
Product knowledge

seiGEN Commerce Lite In Depth

Chapter 7 gave you the basics well enough to run a first visit. This chapter goes further — the depth that separates an RPN who can demo the app from one who can actually solve a vendor's problem with it. Everything below is a feature that's actually built into seiGEN Commerce Lite today, not something planned for later.

Built to work without the internet

Commerce Lite is offline-first: after its very first load, it keeps running without a live connection. For a vendor in an area with patchy signal, that's not a nice-to-have — it's the difference between a tool they can trust every day and one they can't.

Real receipts, from a real printer

The app can talk directly to a thermal receipt printer over WebUSB or Bluetooth, printing a proper receipt without a computer or a print dialog in the way. If a vendor already owns a small mobile receipt printer, Commerce Lite can usually drive it directly.

Getting a vendor's existing stock in fast

Vendors with an existing product list don't need to retype it. The Excel import brings a spreadsheet in directly, matches common column names flexibly, and shows a preview before anything is actually committed — so a mistake in the spreadsheet doesn't become a mistake in their live stock.

Stocktakes done properly

The Stocktake module follows a deliberate three-step pattern: a blind count (so the vendor counts without seeing the old numbers biasing them), a variance report showing what changed, and a separate, explicit step to apply the new count to inventory. Nothing updates silently in the background.

Moving stock between branches

For a vendor running more than one branch, Stock Dispatch and Receive lets stock move from one location to another with a proper record on both ends — and the Product List gives a read-only, cross-branch view with stock levels colour-coded, so a vendor can see what's where at a glance.

Reports that actually answer a question

The Report Writer isn't a wall of numbers — it's filtered, on-screen reports (12 or more types) with a simple sales-trend chart, built so a vendor can find an answer without you in the room. The Sales Report drill-through takes this further: a vendor can open one specific past transaction in a read-only view and produce a clearly watermarked reprint if a customer needs a copy.

Keeping good customers close

Vouchers let a vendor reward frequent customers directly from the app — useful when you're explaining that Commerce Lite isn't just about recording sales, it's about helping them run the business better.

Staff, roles, and who can see what

Staff and Roles lets a vendor bring on employees without handing over full control — Remote branch settings can be passcode-gated, so day-to-day staff can process sales without reaching settings meant for the owner.

FIELD TIP

You don't need to demonstrate every one of these on a first visit. Know they exist, know roughly how they work, and bring them out when a vendor's own question opens the door — that's when they land best.

When Something Breaks

A vendor's confidence in the whole app drops the first time something looks broken. Your job is to stay calm and have the answer ready — not to open the manual in front of them and start searching.

What you'll seeWhat you say and do
Blank screen / won't open“Let's open this in Chrome directly, not the WhatsApp preview.” That fixes it almost every time.
WhatsApp warns the file “may be harmful”“That's normal for this kind of file — it's safe, it's from us.”
Vendor has lost their data“We'll restore it from your last backup — that's exactly what it's there for.” Then go to Settings → Import.
Activation code not acceptedCheck the device code on screen matches exactly what was sent — one digit off will do this.

When you genuinely don't know

Escalate to Digital Commerce rather than guess. A guessed fix that goes wrong costs you more trust than admitting you need to check.

DON'T

Never tell a vendor a broken feature is “coming soon” unless you know that for a fact. If you're not sure, say you'll find out and follow up — then actually do it.

11
Ongoing support

Coordinating Stocktakes

This is where the boundary from Chapter 3 gets practical — and it comes in two sizes. A quick cycle check-in is a light nudge you handle in minutes. A full stocktake job is something a vendor asks you to organize and manage from start to finish. This chapter is about running that second one properly.

Two kinds of stocktake work

KindWhat it looks like
Quick cycle check-inThe vendor counts their own stock on a regular rhythm; you just help capture the result into the app.
Full organized stocktakeA vendor asks Digital Commerce to run a proper stocktake — usually at onboarding, or when their records need a real reset. You organize and manage the whole exercise.

The rest of this chapter is about the full organized stocktake — a chargeable Digital Commerce service, and one of the more important things you'll be trusted to run.

Your job is to organize it, not count everything yourself

When you take on a stocktake job, you're responsible for organizing and managing the exercise — not for personally counting every product. Normally, you hire one stocktake worker, who works alongside a staff member from the vendor's own business. The vendor's staff member matters more than they might seem to: they usually know the stock better than anyone from outside, and can help identify product names, brands, product types, codes, the different names used for the same item, and where things are actually located.

FIELD TIP

Before you agree to a job, be clear in your own head on: what stock needs counting, how big and what type it is, the dates, who's doing the counting, and whether the vendor has a staff member ready who actually knows the stock.

Explain the cost before you start — not after

The stocktake is a chargeable service, and you should explain that when you're onboarding the vendor, not once the counting has already started. The current Digital Commerce rate is US$35 per day — but that's a starting point, not a fixed price for every job. It can move up or down depending on the type of stock, how much of it there is, how difficult the count is, the location of the business, and how much time it will take.

On top of the day rate, the vendor also covers the team's agreed transport and lunches. For a shop outside Harare, the vendor should also arrange boarding for the team where it's needed.

DON'T

Don't let a vendor discover the cost on the day the work starts. Agree the rate, the extras, and who's covering what before anyone begins counting.

Who's on the team, and who pays whom

PersonRolePaid by
Stocktake workerHired to do the physical countDigital Commerce
Vendor's staff memberKnows the stock, helps identify and locate productsThe vendor — not Digital Commerce, for this exercise
You (the RPN)Organizes, manages and verifies the whole exercise—

Agree the hired worker's rate before the job starts, same as everything else in this section.

Give the team the right spreadsheet

Before counting begins, hand the team the approved Digital Commerce stocktake spreadsheet template — not a blank sheet of paper, not a random Excel file. Walk them through what each column means before they start. The spreadsheet isn't just about quantities: it's what maps the vendor's products so the information can be imported into the app correctly.

Search words are worth extra attention

A customer rarely searches using the exact words a vendor uses on the shelf. If a vendor records “Toyota Corolla Front Brake Pad”, a customer might search “Corolla brake pads”, “Toyota brake pads”, or just “front pads”. Make sure the team captures the useful, everyday words a real customer would type — not just the vendor's own shorthand. That's what iTred VPD uses to actually help a customer find the product later.

FIELD TIP

A stocktake isn't only a counting exercise — it's a product-data exercise. Good search words captured now are what make a product findable later.

During the physical count

For every product, make sure the team is capturing: the correct name, the brand where it applies, the product code or SKU where one exists, the correct quantity, a short description where required, and useful search words. If anyone on the team genuinely doesn't know what an item is, the answer is to ask the vendor's staff member — never to guess.

Big stock? Work in batches

A large stocktake doesn't have to happen all in one sitting. Split it by category — Batch 1, Batch 2, and so on. For each batch: check the physical count, check the spreadsheet, correct any errors, let the relevant stakeholders verify it, get sign-off, and upload that batch where the agreed process allows it — then move to the next one.

Verify before you upload anything

Once the count and the spreadsheet are done, don't upload immediately. Check first: was everything actually counted, are the quantities right, are names and codes correct, are the search words useful, are there duplicates or missing products, and have any differences been looked into? Only once everyone involved is satisfied should the work be signed off.

DON'T

Don't upload a spreadsheet just because the counting is finished. Bad data imported into the POS creates bad stock records and poor product search — for the vendor and for iTred VPD.

You personally upload the approved file

Once the spreadsheet is the final, approved, signed-off version, you — the RPN — are the one who uploads it into the app. Confirm it's genuinely the final version, confirm sign-off happened, do the upload, then check that the information actually landed correctly. If something didn't import right, report or correct it before you consider the job done.

Cycle stocktakes: keeping it accurate over time

A stocktake done once, on day one, only tells you the picture was accurate on day one. Cycle stocktakes — arranged afterwards for audit and stock-control purposes — are what keep it accurate going forward. A cycle stocktake doesn't have to mean counting the whole shop again; you can agree with the vendor to check selected products, selected categories, or investigate a specific difference.

The process, start to finish

  1. Discuss the stocktake with the vendor
  2. Explain the charges and arrangements
  3. Agree the dates and requirements
  4. Organize the hired stocktake worker
  5. Arrange for a knowledgeable vendor staff member to take part
  6. Give the team the Digital Commerce spreadsheet
  7. Conduct the physical count
  8. Correctly map products and capture search words
  9. Complete the spreadsheet
  10. Verify the completed work
  11. Obtain stakeholder sign-off
  12. Upload the approved spreadsheet into the app
  13. Check the imported information
  14. Arrange cycle stocktakes when required
FIELD TIP

Whatever else changes from job to job, that order doesn't: agree, organize, count, map, verify, sign off, upload, check, repeat on a cycle.
